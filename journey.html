The long way
round

How I ended up building AI for problems that matter

When I was ten, I read about a pancreatic cancer vaccine being developed abroad, and something clicked. I'd lost my father to that same cancer, but reading that, I stopped seeing it as just my story and started seeing it as one of countless stories: patients everywhere losing their lives to diseases we don't yet know how to treat in time. That was the day I decided to spend my life on science with a purpose, using technology to solve the kinds of problems that cause that much pain.

The path was anything but linear. I wanted to be a genetic engineer, chased the medical entrance exams, fell short, and briefly studied microbiology before realising I couldn't do biology without the math and logic I loved. I landed in computer science on a full scholarship and slowly saw it was the better door into the same room: I could bring science and medicine into code instead of the other way around. Then machine learning clicked, and years later AI made the whole thing feel possible in a way it never had before. To me, it's the most powerful tool we've ever built, and I want to use it for the problems that actually matter.

The thread hasn't changed since. I've published research, filed a patent application, and kept picking problems worth solving. And somewhere along the way I learned to trust myself. I've switched fields with a lot of catching up to do, started in technical support barely knowing what a localhost was, and become the person who built the team's first training program. I don't expect to be the most prepared person in the room on day one. I just know that if I put my head down, I'll get there, usually further than I expected.

What I care about most is trust.

In anything that affects real people, a confident wrong answer is worse than an honest "I don't know."

That comes straight from where I started: no one should suffer through the wrong answer when the right one was possible. I'd rather ship something reliable than something that just looks good on a slide, and it shows up in the work: a no-call option when the genomic evidence is weak, flagging small-sample overfitting instead of publishing a perfect-looking score. Today I'm at Penn, building and evaluating AI products, and chasing the same thing I've wanted since I was a kid: to build technology that genuinely helps people.
