My Journey

Why I build what I build · how I like to work

When I was ten, I read about a pancreatic cancer vaccine being developed abroad, and something clicked. I'd lost my father to that same cancer, but reading that, I stopped seeing it as just my story and started seeing it as one of countless stories: patients everywhere losing their lives to diseases we don't yet know how to treat in time. That was the day I decided to spend my life on science with a purpose, using technology to solve the kinds of problems that cause that much pain. I couldn't stop wondering how something like that gets made, and what else we could make.

The path was anything but linear. I wanted to be a genetic engineer, chased the medical entrance exams, fell short, and briefly studied microbiology before realising I couldn't do biology without the math and logic I loved. I landed in computer science on a full scholarship and, honestly, assumed I'd left medicine behind. Then machine learning clicked, and years later AI did something I never expected: it took me closer to healthcare, not further from it. I could bring science and medicine into code instead of the other way around. To me, it's the most powerful tool we've ever built, and I want to use it for the problems that actually matter.

That's where the work began. I built a model to predict which Parkinson's patients respond to levodopa, which became a paper, a review article and a patent application, and my first real taste of asking whether a model can tell you something early enough to act on it.

Technical support taught me the other half. I started barely knowing what a localhost was and became the person who built the team's first training program. Owning customer escalations showed me where real users get stuck and what a wrong answer costs them, and I worked with engineering, sales and product teams to turn those pain points into shipped fixes. I don't expect to be the most prepared person in the room on day one. I just know that if I put my head down, I'll get there, usually further than I expected.

Now it's coming together, and I'm back in research after five years away. At Penn I'm a Wharton AI-PMT Fellow building Career Canvas, and a research assistant in Prof. Kartik Hosanagar's lab, building a harness where an AI judge has to quote its evidence and code checks the quote is real. I'm doing my master's thesis in Kevin Johnson's lab on speech recognition that runs inside the exam room. My first research project in the US, on compressing whole-slide pathology images, won Best Research Poster at SIIM CAIMI26, in a room of pathologists, radiologists and cardiologists where I was the computer scientist.

I keep following the same question into new places: whether a pancreatic cancer will resist its chemotherapy, which antibiotic will fail before the lab results arrive. Each field has its own vocabulary, and I've learned to enjoy being the newcomer. Every time, the hard part isn't the model. It's deciding how sure to sound: our genome model has a no-call option for when the evidence is weak, and our write-up flagged a near-perfect score on a small sample as a warning sign, not a result.

What I care about most is trust.

In anything that affects real people, a confident wrong answer is worse than an honest "I don't know."

I started out a believer in technology. The work taught me that belief isn't enough: AI has to earn trust, by showing its evidence, saying "I don't know" when it doesn't, handing off to a person when it should, and being tested before anyone claims it works. That's the part I build, and it comes straight from where I started: no one should suffer through the wrong answer when the right one was possible. Healthcare is where I started and still the hardest test, but the question follows me anywhere a wrong answer has a cost: a pathology slide, a parts order, a brand post, a course plan. I've been the newcomer in every room I've worked in, and I wouldn't trade it. I feel like I'm in the right place at the right time, ready for the next challenge, and I want to be part of this AI era the way I've wanted since I was ten: to build technology that genuinely helps people.
